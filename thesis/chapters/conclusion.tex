\chapter*{CONCLUSION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}
\markboth{CONCLUSION}{CONCLUSION}

This thesis set out from an ordinary observation: when an object is lost, the
information needed to recover it usually already exists, split between two
people who have no way of finding each other. The channels available today ---
unconnected lost-property desks, social-media groups where a report survives a
few hours, word of mouth --- fail for one structural reason rather than through
any lack of goodwill. Nothing in them is responsible for comparing a loss report
against a discovery report.

\projectname{} assigns that responsibility to software. We designed and built a
platform on which a single guided report enters a national pool that is searched
automatically and continuously, in both directions. The comparison is multimodal
and multilingual: descriptions are encoded by a multilingual sentence model, so
that a report written in Arabic is comparable with one written in French or
English, and photographs are encoded by a vision-language model and searched as
an independent channel, so that a clear picture attached to three words of text
still reaches the right owner. Both vector spaces live inside PostgreSQL through
the \code{pgvector} extension, which allows approximate nearest-neighbour
retrieval and relational filtering to be expressed as one query rather than
stitched together in application code.

Building the engine taught more than designing it did. The instructive failure
was silent: raw similarities returned by the vision model are not calibrated
probabilities, and two entirely unrelated photographs still score around $0.7$
--- enough, when fed directly into a weighted average, to push unrelated objects
in front of users while every unit test continued to pass. Correcting it
required rescaling the similarity against a measured floor, redistributing
fusion weights over the modalities actually present so that a missing photograph
is never read as a dissimilar one, and blending a lexical term so that
\dq{iPhone~14} and \dq{iPhone~13} stop being interchangeable. Each of those
corrections came from examining outputs rather than from reading documentation.

The other conclusion is that accuracy alone would not have produced a usable
product. A high-confidence match is a hypothesis about two objects, not an
authorisation to disclose a person's telephone number, and the platform
therefore separates the two completely: recovery passes through verification
questions set by the reporter, answered by the claimant, and judged by the
reporter, with contact details exchanged only after approval. Users are shown
not just a percentage but the reasons behind it, translated into their own
language, so that they can apply judgement the system does not have. These are
product decisions rather than machine-learning ones, and they determine whether
anyone is willing to report a found object at all.

Developed within the framework of the university, this project is presented both
as an engineering result and as a viable startup, whose market, organisation and
financial structure are analysed in the preceding chapters.

\section*{Future work}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Future work}

Several directions remain open, and the system was built to accommodate them
without structural change:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Learned scoring.} Every confirmed or rejected suggestion is
        already persisted as a labelled example alongside the scores that
        produced it. That dataset is exactly what is needed to replace the
        hand-set weights and boosts with a calibrated model, learned globally
        and per category --- photographs should dominate for bags and luggage,
        text for documents and keys.

  \item \textbf{Cross-modal matching.} Comparing the words of one report against
        the photograph of another, which the vision-language model already
        supports and which would rescue pairs where one side has only text and
        the other only an image.

  \item \textbf{Field validation.} Deploying with a pilot institution and
        measuring real recovery rates, which is the only measurement that
        ultimately matters and cannot be obtained from a demonstration corpus.

  \item \textbf{Institutional integration.} Allowing partner lost-property desks
        to publish their registers directly into the shared pool.

  \item \textbf{Native mobile application}, enabling a report to be filed from
        the camera at the moment of discovery.

  \item \textbf{Trust and safety at scale.} Reputation signals and moderation
        tooling for disputed claims, which become necessary as volume grows.
\end{itemize}
